The Sunday Forecast measures not what is said about Jesus, but what is doing the theological work in the message.

This is not a ranking of good churches or faithful pastors. It measures one thing: how clearly the finished work carried the message this week. A sermon can be warm, true, and helpful and still read cloudy here, because the Forecast is looking for one specific thing.

Grace Present, but Response Governed

This Week in One Sentence

For the first time since this index began, a sermon reached clear skies, and it showed what the other nine were missing: not more grace said, but grace that held the whole message up.

The Patterns

Visibility

Visibility came in at 51, partly cloudy, one point above last week. Ten sermons scored, spanning 28 to 92. One reading reached clear skies, five sat partly cloudy, and four settled overcast. None landed mostly clear and none in storm. The average barely moved, but the shape of the board changed: the top of the scale was reached for the first time, while the rest of the board stayed close to where it has sat all season.

The Governing Pattern

Proclamation again outran governance, which is the pattern this index keeps finding. Union and identity read strongest among the proclamation items this week; most sermons told believers, somewhere, who they are in Christ. But in nine of ten sermons, something other than the finished work carried the structure. Growth through community. Surrender to the authority of Scripture. Alignment to design. Rehearsing true thoughts. Release after a hard season. Vocation. Courage to take hold of a promise. Generosity. The church’s own mandate. Each of these is a real good. None of them is the foundation the gospel lays, and in each sermon the supply, not the Savior, bore the weight.

Nine of Ten Would Still Stand

The Load-Bearing Test asks whether a sermon’s argument still stands once every statement about the finished work is removed. This week nine of ten would have stood. The landing, which asks where the listener is left at the close, tells the same story from the other end: nine sermons sent people out to activate, attempt, or supply, and one sent them out resting in what was already done. These two items were again the weakest on the board, and the distance between them and the proclamation items is the whole diagnosis. The gospel was said in most of these pulpits. It was built on in one.

The Bottom of the Board

Four readings landed below 45, all in overcast. The lowest, at 28, carried a contradiction flag, this index’s marker for a message that does not merely underplay the finished work but teaches against it somewhere. In it, the congregation’s calling and spiritual mandate carried the argument, the language of Christ’s sacrifice served that calling rather than grounding the listener’s standing, and the believer’s security before God was never stated as settled. The other three were earnest and practical, each with a gospel moment that came late or ran thin, while vocation, courage, or generosity did the structural work. None of these sermons was careless. Each asked the listener to supply something the gospel says was already supplied.

What Clear Skies Looked Like

One sermon read 92, the first reading on this index to cross into clear skies, and it is worth describing precisely. It did not simply say more about grace than the others. It addressed believers and unbelievers distinctly from the start, so one group heard their standing as settled fact and the other heard an invitation rather than a demand. It located the believer’s standing as something already possessed in Christ, not something to be pursued. Every practical exhortation that followed was framed as the fruit of that standing rather than the way to secure it. And it closed with the listener resting in what was already true. Take the finished work out of that sermon and very little is left standing. That is what this index exists to look for, and this week it found it.

The difference was not how much grace was said. It was whether anything was built on it.

The Editorial

A clear-sky reading is not a trophy, and this index does not hand out awards. What it offers is a proof of concept. For months the Forecast has argued that the gap between proclaiming the finished work and building on it is a matter of blueprint, not belief. This week one of the world’s most-watched pulpits closed that gap, which means the gap was never a law of preaching. Nearly every preacher on this board believes what he or she said about the cross. The invitation is simply to let it carry the weight.

The Index

The Grace Clarity Index (GCI) measures how clearly a sermon presents the finished work of Christ as the foundation of the believer's identity, security, and righteousness. Each sermon is scored on ten items across three dimensions: what it proclaims, what governs its structure, and how it handles the text and the room. The composite is simple arithmetic: two times the sum of the ten items. It does not evaluate sincerity or salvation.

The Index reads every sermon through one lens, and it holds that lens openly. It asks whether the believer's standing was grounded in what Christ finished, or in what the listener was sent away to do. That is a New Covenant standard, and it is not neutral. A preacher from another tradition would not so much fail this index as decline its premise. So a low reading is a diagnosis, not a verdict. It does not measure sincerity, gifting, or whether a life was changed in that room. It measures one current running through the week's teaching, and nothing more.
Calibration check: the Index is periodically run against preachers known for explicit finished-work teaching to confirm it can register clear skies. It can.
