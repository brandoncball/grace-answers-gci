The Sunday Forecast measures not what is said about Jesus, but what is doing the theological work in the message.

This is not a ranking of good churches or faithful pastors. It measures one thing: how clearly the finished work carried the message this week. A sermon can be warm, true, and helpful and still read cloudy here, because the Forecast is looking for one specific thing.

This Week in One Sentence

Almost every sermon this week carried a real moment of gospel proclamation — and then set it down and built the message on the listener’s next move.

The Patterns

Visibility

Visibility closed at 49, partly cloudy, five points up from last week’s low-water mark. The lift came from the top: after a week in which nothing rose above the mid-fifties, one sermon reached 66 and crossed into mostly clear — the first reading in that band in nearly a month. The rest of the board sat where it usually sits, four readings in the partly cloudy band and three under it, with the floor at 36. The week was not clearer so much as taller: the same middle, a recovered ceiling.

The Governing Pattern

Six of the eight sermons scored this week share a single architecture, and it is worth naming precisely. Each one contains a genuinely strong gospel moment — a debt declared paid, a righteousness declared given, a union declared real. And in each one that moment sits inside a structure that runs on something else: perseverance through disappointment, resolve, confession, openness, mission. The proclamation is an interlude. The structure is an engine, and the engine is the listener. The gap between the two dimensions tells the story in numbers: what the sermons said about Christ averaged just under six out of ten; what actually carried their weight averaged under three and a half. That distance is the week’s diagnosis — not absence, displacement.

The Recovered Ceiling

The week’s highest reading earned its band the honest way: completion and union were not only stated but developed, at full marks on both. What kept it out of clear skies is the same thing that keeps nearly everything out of clear skies — the structure underneath. Its problem, its turning points, and its landing still ran on rescue experienced rather than righteousness accomplished. The instrument paid the proclamation everything it was worth and could not pay the architecture what it never earned. That is the profile of the whole era this index keeps reading: the gospel is welcome in the room and absent from the load-bearing walls.

The Bottom of the Board

The lowest reading of the week, at 36, is also the starkest version of the pattern inverted: a warm, pastorally skilled message about coming home to a welcoming Father in which the finished work of Christ never appears — not weakly stated, absent. The welcome was real and the ground under it was never named. And the week’s weakest single item, for the second straight week, was the load-bearing test: remove the grace statements from these sermons and, in most of them, the argument still stands. A sermon that survives the removal of its gospel was not resting on it.

Where the Weight Landed

The landing tells the same story from the final minutes. Across the week, listeners were overwhelmingly sent rather than settled: sent to persevere, to confess, to yield, to decide, to go. Assurance was the strongest item on the board — these sermons genuinely told believers they are secure — but the security was rarely allowed to carry the ending. The strongest problem-statements of the week were about circumstances; almost none posed the problem the cross exists to answer. When the problem is disappointment and the landing is resolve, the middle can quote all the grace it likes: the message has already chosen its foundation.

The gospel was welcome in the room and absent from the load-bearing walls.

The Editorial

There is a difference between a sermon that contains the finished work and a sermon that is carried by it, and this week made that difference unusually visible. The proclamation numbers this week were the strongest in over a month; the governance numbers barely moved. That combination is not hypocrisy — it is habit. Preachers reach for the cross as consolation inside a message whose real machinery is the listener’s response, because that machinery is what the form rewards. But the New Testament runs the other direction: the indicative is the architecture, not the ornament. What Christ finished is not the encouraging aside on the way to the application. It is the ground the application stands on — or the application is standing on the listener.

The Index

The Grace Clarity Index (GCI) measures how clearly a sermon presents the finished work of Christ as the foundation of the believer's identity, security, and righteousness. Each sermon is scored on ten items across three dimensions: what it proclaims, what governs its structure, and how it handles the text and the room. The composite is simple arithmetic: two times the sum of the ten items. It does not evaluate sincerity or salvation.

The Index reads every sermon through one lens, and it holds that lens openly. It asks whether the believer's standing was grounded in what Christ finished, or in what the listener was sent away to do. That is a New Covenant standard, and it is not neutral. A preacher from another tradition would not so much fail this index as decline its premise. So a low reading is a diagnosis, not a verdict. It does not measure sincerity, gifting, or whether a life was changed in that room. It measures one current running through the week's teaching, and nothing more.
Calibration check: the Index is periodically run against preachers known for explicit finished-work teaching to confirm it can register clear skies. It can.
