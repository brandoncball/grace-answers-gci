The Finished Work Was Present but Not the Foundation

This Week in One Sentence

The sky closed in this week: every sermon still said something true about the cross, and then handed the listener a shift, a step, a story, or a song to carry the ending.

The Patterns

Visibility

Visibility fell to 44, overcast, seven points under last week’s reading and the lowest edition since early August. Nine sermons scored, and the spread narrowed to thirty points, from 30 to 60. Nothing reached mostly clear. Four readings sat partly cloudy and five sat overcast, which means that for the first time in a month, more of the board lived below the midpoint than above it. The drop did not come from one bad sermon dragging the average down. It came from the whole board sliding a few points at once, and from three readings settling at 30 and 34, where the finished work was an appeal near the front and nothing more.

The Governing Pattern

The pattern that governed the week is familiar, but it sharpened. In sermon after sermon a real gospel moment appeared, was stated warmly and in most cases accurately, and was then set down while the message built its actual structure on what the listener would do next. The something-next varied more than usual this week: discerning the Spirit and healing from trauma, holding on to and singing a song, waking up and repositioning, becoming dependent on the Spirit, sharing a personal story and signing a commitment, choosing to be a blessing, shifting into a promise, evaluating oneself and taking the next step. The architecture underneath did not vary at all. Proclamation averaged a little over five out of ten per item; governance averaged barely three. The gap between what the sermons said about Christ and what actually carried them is as wide as any this index has recorded.

Faith Changed Its Object

The item that moved most this week was faith. Last edition it was the second-strongest item on the board; this week it fell to the bottom third, and four of the nine sermons scored it at the lowest rung above absent. In those four, faith was no longer described as trust in what Christ accomplished. It functioned as the listener’s instrument: the thing you activate, the declaration you make, the shift you choose, the decision that unlocks what God is waiting to release. The Index reads that not as a weaker version of the gospel but as a different mechanism altogether, because when faith becomes the lever, the finished work becomes the reward at the end of it rather than the ground underneath it. Assurance, meanwhile, was still the strongest item on the board even as it slipped: listeners were told they are secure and loved, often movingly. Security was declared in the middle of these sermons and then handed back to the listener’s effort at the close.

The Bottom of the Board

Three sermons landed at the bottom of the board, at 30, 30, and 34, and they share one feature the Index weighs heavily: their closing minutes contained no declaration of anything already finished. The landing item scored zero in all three. One sent the room to shift into a prophetic promise, one to choose blessing through declaration, one to evaluate itself and climb to the next step. In each, the gospel was present as an appeal near the front and absent as a foundation at the end. Remove the grace statements from any of the three and the sermon stands untouched. That is the load-bearing test, and it is the single clearest diagnostic the Index runs: grace that can be removed without collapse was never holding the building up.

Where the Weight Landed

The landing was the weakest number on the board at 2.4 out of ten, the same reading as the late-August edition. Three sermons ended with no declaration at all, the other six ended on a soft activation with a grace phrase attached, and none closed at rest. The problem statements ran the same direction. Not one sermon this week posed the problem that the cross exists to answer; every one posed a life problem, drift, chaos, waiting, silence, a lack of peace, and the solution offered was, in every case, a mixture of God’s help and the listener’s participation. When the problem is a life problem, the solution will almost always be a life practice, and the finished work will almost always end up as encouragement on the way to it.

Grace that can be removed without collapse was never holding the building up.

The Editorial

It is worth saying plainly what an overcast week is and is not. It is not a week of bad theology in the sense of false statements; the cross was described accurately in nearly every room this week, and several of these sermons would be a comfort to a hurting person. It is a week in which the true things said about Christ did not do the work. The New Testament’s pattern runs the other way: the indicative is the architecture, and the imperative is what a person does once the building is already standing. What this week’s board shows, more sharply than most, is how easily that order inverts when the form of the sermon is built to produce a response. The response becomes the frame, the finished work becomes the furniture, and faith, which was meant to be the hand that receives, becomes the hand that reaches. The fix is not more grace sentences. It is a different question at the desk on Tuesday: not what do I want them to do, but what is already true of them, and does this sermon collapse if I take it out.

The Index

The Grace Clarity Index (GCI) measures how clearly a sermon presents the finished work of Christ as the foundation of the believer's identity, security, and righteousness. Each sermon is scored on ten items across three dimensions: what it proclaims, what governs its structure, and how it handles the text and the room. The composite is simple arithmetic: two times the sum of the ten items. It does not evaluate sincerity or salvation.

The Index reads every sermon through one lens, and it holds that lens openly. It asks whether the believer's standing was grounded in what Christ finished, or in what the listener was sent away to do. That is a New Covenant standard, and it is not neutral. A preacher from another tradition would not so much fail this index as decline its premise. So a low reading is a diagnosis, not a verdict. It does not measure sincerity, gifting, or whether a life was changed in that room. It measures one current running through the week's teaching, and nothing more.
Calibration check: the Index is periodically run against preachers known for explicit finished-work teaching to confirm it can register clear skies. It can.
