This Week in One Sentence

The sky held almost exactly where it was, but the board tightened around a single finding: nine of ten sermons named the finished work and then built on something else, and the one that climbed did it by making union with Christ the frame rather than the opening line.

The Patterns

Visibility

Visibility came in at 47, partly cloudy, one point above last week and the third consecutive reading in the same band. Ten sermons scored, spanning 34 to 70. One reading reached mostly clear, four sat partly cloudy, five settled overcast, and nothing stormed. The movement week to week is now small enough to be noise; what is not noise is the shape underneath it, which has not changed in a month. This index keeps reading the same sermon written ten different ways.

The Governing Pattern

Proclamation averaged 21 of a possible 40 and governance 14. The gap is the diagnosis and it has been the diagnosis all season: these pulpits say more about Christ than their arguments are built on. In every one of the ten, a genuine gospel sentence appeared. It was warm, it was usually accurate, and it was then set down while the message built its actual frame on something the listener would supply. The supplied thing varied widely. Receptivity and movement in one. Obedience and meditation in another. Purification to unlock a promise. Self-regulation of thought. Integrated work life. Next steps and mission participation. Wisdom applied and surrender offered. Seeing, sowing, sharing. Ten different supplies, one architecture.

The Landing Has Not Moved in Five Editions

The landing came in at 3.2 of ten and is the weakest item on the board for the fifth edition running, with the load-bearing test just beneath it at 3.0, its lowest reading of the season. Those two items ask the plainest questions the instrument has. At the final minute, is the listener left resting in something already finished, or activating something not yet done? And if every finished-work sentence were removed, would the sermon still stand? Nine of the ten closed on activation. In eight of them the building survives the removal untouched. That second number is the harder one. It means the grace language was not load-bearing; it was decorative, and the sermon was engineered to work without it.

The Bottom of the Board

Five readings landed overcast between 34 and 44, and three of the ten carried a contradiction flag, this index’s marker for a message that does not merely underplay the finished work but teaches against it somewhere. In one, a real cleansing-by-Christ moment was folded into a transaction in which the listener purifies himself so that a promise can be released. In another, encounter with the word through obedience and meditation took over the structural role the cross had briefly occupied. In a third, warm identity language sat inside a frame where integrated effort, not accomplished redemption, carried the weight. The problem in all three is not missing vocabulary. It is that the vocabulary was present and did no work.

What the Top Reading Did Differently

One sermon read 70, mostly clear, and the way it got there is worth stating precisely, because it is the most instructive data point in the edition. It did not win on warmth or on volume of gospel language. It won on placement. Union with Christ was not the opening move before the real sermon started; it was the frame the rest of the message was hung on, and the cross supplied actual release from actual guilt rather than a sentiment about worth. Its proclamation scored 34 of 40. Its governance scored 30, and the 10-point gap is where it stopped short: the structure still leaned toward ability and progress in the back half. The distance between 70 and 44 this week is not sincerity, skill, or conviction. It is whether the finished work is the floor or the doorway.

The vocabulary was present and did no work.

The Editorial

An index that reads partly cloudy three weeks running invites a fair question: is the standard simply unreachable? The answer this week is no, and the evidence is the top of the board. A sermon can name the finished work, develop it, and let it carry the turns, and one did. But the more useful observation is about the other nine, and it is structural rather than doctrinal. When a message is designed to produce a response, the response becomes the architecture and the finished work becomes the furniture inside it. That is not a failure of belief. Every preacher on this board almost certainly believes the gospel he stated. It is a failure of load-bearing, and it is invisible from inside the sermon, because the grace sentences are all there and all true. The test that exposes it takes one minute: take them out, and see whether anything falls.

The Index

The Grace Clarity Index (GCI) measures how clearly a sermon presents the finished work of Christ as the foundation of the believer's identity, security, and righteousness. Each sermon is scored on ten items across three dimensions: what it proclaims, what governs its structure, and how it handles the text and the room. The composite is simple arithmetic: two times the sum of the ten items. It does not evaluate sincerity or salvation.

The Index reads every sermon through one lens, and it holds that lens openly. It asks whether the believer's standing was grounded in what Christ finished, or in what the listener was sent away to do. That is a New Covenant standard, and it is not neutral. A preacher from another tradition would not so much fail this index as decline its premise. So a low reading is a diagnosis, not a verdict. It does not measure sincerity, gifting, or whether a life was changed in that room. It measures one current running through the week's teaching, and nothing more.
Calibration check: the Index is periodically run against preachers known for explicit finished-work teaching to confirm it can register clear skies. It can.
