This Week in One Sentence

The gospel was spoken more clearly this week than it has been in a month, and not one sermon was built on it.

The Patterns

Visibility

Visibility closed at 51, partly cloudy, four points above the previous edition. The gain is real and it is worth naming plainly: the sermons said more about Christ this week, and they said it better. One reading reached mostly clear, the first time the top of the board has cleared 60 in a fortnight, and nothing fell into storm. But the spread tells the more interesting story. Nine sermons, a twenty-six point range, and seven of the nine still clustered in the middle band. The week improved at the level of what was said, and did not move at the level of what was carrying it.

The Governing Pattern

Nine sermons were scored this week and nine of them share a single architecture. In each one a genuine gospel moment appears, sometimes briefly and sometimes at real length, and in each one that moment sits inside a structure governed by something else: providence, spiritual presence, prayer, community, evangelistic resolve, promise, calling, personal response. The moment is not fake. Several were substantial statements of imputed righteousness and substitution, the kind of sentence that would have anchored the whole message if the message had been built to receive it. It was not. What the sermons proclaimed averaged just over six out of ten per item; what actually governed their structure averaged under four. Grace was articulate this week and it was still furniture.

The Load-Bearing Test

One item on this index asks a question that cannot be answered with warmth: remove every grace statement from the sermon, and does the sermon collapse? Nothing on the board this week scored above a two out of five on that question. Not the best reading, not the worst, not one of the nine. Every sermon scored this week survives the removal of its own gospel content, because the gospel content was never the thing holding it up. That is a structural finding rather than a tonal one, and it is why the average can rise four points while the diagnosis stays exactly where it was. You can improve every sentence about Christ in a message without changing what the message runs on.

The Bottom of the Board

The two lowest readings, at 40 and 42, both landed overcast, and one of them shows the pattern at its thinnest. In that message the vocabulary of the gospel is present throughout, but the believer’s identity in Christ is essentially absent as a category: the sermon is about restored hope and providential encouragement, and the cross is referenced without ever being the mechanism of anything. The scoring flagged an internal contradiction, which is what happens when a message affirms a truth in one breath and builds on a different foundation in the next. Gospel words are not the same as a gospel structure, and this week the difference was measurable.

Two Sermons That Landed

The endings deserve credit where credit is due. Seven of the nine sermons closed by sending the listener somewhere, which is the ordinary pattern this index reads week after week. Two did not. One closed on communion, letting the table say what the sermon had been circling. Another let an overruling decree stand as the last word rather than converting it into an assignment. Those two endings are the reason the landing average nearly doubled from the previous edition, and they are worth studying precisely because they were not softer or vaguer than the others. They were more specific. Resting a listener takes more theological precision than mobilizing one, not less.

Nine sermons named the finished work this week. Not one was built on it.

The Editorial

There is a temptation, reading a week like this one, to call it progress and move on. The numbers went up. The gospel was clearer. That is genuinely good, and it should be said before anything else is said. But the New Testament does not treat the finished work as content to be included well. It treats it as the ground everything else stands on, which is why Paul can spend eleven chapters on what God has done before he asks anyone to do anything. When the indicative is the architecture, the imperative has somewhere to rest. When the indicative is an excellent paragraph inside a building framed by demand, the listener hears the demand, because the demand is what the structure was shaped to deliver. This week the paragraphs got better. The building did not change.

The Index

The Grace Clarity Index (GCI) measures how clearly a sermon presents the finished work of Christ as the foundation of the believer's identity, security, and righteousness. Each sermon is scored on ten items across three dimensions: what it proclaims, what governs its structure, and how it handles the text and the room. The composite is simple arithmetic: two times the sum of the ten items. It does not evaluate sincerity or salvation.

The Index reads every sermon through one lens, and it holds that lens openly. It asks whether the believer's standing was grounded in what Christ finished, or in what the listener was sent away to do. That is a New Covenant standard, and it is not neutral. A preacher from another tradition would not so much fail this index as decline its premise. So a low reading is a diagnosis, not a verdict. It does not measure sincerity, gifting, or whether a life was changed in that room. It measures one current running through the week's teaching, and nothing more.
Calibration check: the Index is periodically run against preachers known for explicit finished-work teaching to confirm it can register clear skies. It can.
