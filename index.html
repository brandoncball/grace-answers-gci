Grace Present, but Response Governed

Six sermons from the world’s most-watched pulpits, read for one thing: how clearly the finished work of Christ governed the message.

The Sunday Forecast measures not what is said about Jesus, but what is doing the theological work in the message.

This is not a ranking of good churches or faithful pastors. It measures one thing: how clearly the finished work carried the message this week. A sermon can be warm, true, and helpful and still read cloudy here, because the Forecast is looking for one specific thing.

This Week in One Sentence

This week the board narrowed and the old pattern held: the finished work was stated warmly in the room, and the sermon was built on something else.

The Patterns

Visibility

Visibility came in at 46, partly cloudy, down two points from last week, on a board of six sermons. Scores ranged from 26 to 56, a spread of thirty points, the widest in weeks. Nothing reached mostly clear for the fourth week running. Five sat partly cloudy, one settled overcast, and nothing stormed. Four further readings were held out of the board because their runs did not agree closely enough to state with confidence. A wide spread with a flat middle means the board has a floor but no ceiling.

The Governing Pattern

Proclamation again outran governance, and by a wider margin than usual. Union with Christ and assurance read well: in most sermons the listener was told, plainly, that they were accepted and secure. But the question this index keeps asking is not what was said. It is what carried the weight. Courage and spiritual authority carried one message. Generosity and provision carried another. Obedience, handoff, and the discipline of a daily practice carried others. Each is a real good, and in each sermon the assignment, not the Savior, bore the load.

Where the Listener Was Left

The Landing was again the lowest item on the board, and by some distance. The Landing asks where the listener is left at the close, resting in what is finished or sent out to do something about it. In nearly every sermon this week the close was an instruction: resist, give, return, practice, show up. Consider a sermon that made a clear and sincere statement of the cross, then spent its final minutes on what the room should do by Friday. Both were true. Only one of them was where the listener was left.

The Bottom of the Board

One reading landed overcast, and it was a thin one. The finished work appeared only as a brief reference to salvation, and the weight of the message rested on practices, expectation, and a hoped-for experience. Separately, one of the partly cloudy readings carried a contradiction flag, this index’s marker for a message that does not merely underplay the finished work but teaches against it somewhere. There the listener’s own response was set up as the test that determines what follows. Neither message was careless. Each asked the listener to supply something the gospel says was already supplied.

The Bright Spot

The top reading was 56, and it deserves a plain description. It stated the cross and the believer’s acceptance clearly and more than once, and the instrument credited it. What held it below mostly clear is a feature of the instrument, not a fault of the preacher: the structure of the message was a movement toward rest through something the listener was to practice, with the finished work serving as the encouragement rather than the foundation. Comfort that sits beside the structure is not the same as comfort that is the structure. It was still the strongest reading on the board.

A sermon can hold the gospel in one hand and hand the listener a task with the other.

The Editorial

The pattern this week is not neglect. The cross and the resurrection were named in nearly every pulpit, and several named them with real warmth. The pattern is what the message was built on. When the gospel is a section of the sermon rather than its foundation, the listener hears good news and then leaves with a method, and the method is what goes home. The invitation to every preacher on this board is not to say more about grace. It is to ask, before the first point, what will still be standing if I take the cross out of this message, and to let the honest answer be nothing.

The Index

The Grace Clarity Index (GCI) measures how clearly a sermon presents the finished work of Christ as the foundation of the believer's identity, security, and righteousness. Each sermon is scored on ten items across three dimensions: what it proclaims, what governs its structure, and how it handles the text and the room. The composite is simple arithmetic: two times the sum of the ten items. It does not evaluate sincerity or salvation.

The Index reads every sermon through one lens, and it holds that lens openly. It asks whether the believer's standing was grounded in what Christ finished, or in what the listener was sent away to do. That is a New Covenant standard, and it is not neutral. A preacher from another tradition would not so much fail this index as decline its premise. So a low reading is a diagnosis, not a verdict. It does not measure sincerity, gifting, or whether a life was changed in that room. It measures one current running through the week's teaching, and nothing more.
Calibration check: the Index is periodically run against preachers known for explicit finished-work teaching to confirm it can register clear skies. It can.
